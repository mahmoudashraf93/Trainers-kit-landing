Goals are useful when clients can revisit and adjust them as life changes. A goal written on an intake form deserves a conversation throughout the coaching relationship.

Effective goal-setting is a collaborative, iterative process. The worksheet below gives you a structured way to co-design goals with clients, anticipate barriers, and build in a deliberate revision cycle.

---

## Two Levels of Goals

Before using the worksheet, it helps to distinguish between two types of goals:

**Outcome goals** describe a result the client wants: "I want to run a 5K," "I want to feel less tired," "I want to carry my shopping without my back aching."

**Behavior goals** describe the actions that lead there: "I will attend two sessions per week," "I will walk for 20 minutes on off days," "I will do the mobility routine we agreed on three mornings a week."

Clients may arrive with outcome goals. Your job is to help them build behavior goals that are actually achievable given their real life — not their aspirational life. The [CDC's physical activity guidelines for adults](https://www.cdc.gov/physical-activity-basics/guidelines/adults.html) can provide a useful reference point for what frequency and volume of movement tends to support general health outcomes, though every client's starting point is different.

## Worked Example

**Client:** Tom, 46, software engineer. His intake form says his goal is "lose weight," but in the consultation he described wanting to "feel less physically useless — I can't keep up with my kids and it embarrasses me."

**Trainer's move:** Reframe with Tom, not for Tom.

---

**PART 1 — OUTCOME GOAL**

*What do you most want to achieve?*
To be able to play with my kids for a full afternoon without needing to sit down. Ideally also feel stronger when I pick them up.

*How meaningful: 9/10*

*Why it matters:* I don't want to be the dad who watches from the bench.

*By when:* I'd like to feel a noticeable difference by Christmas (approx. 8 weeks).

---

**PART 2 — BEHAVIOR GOALS**

Goal 1: Attend two personal training sessions per week.
How often: Twice weekly. When: Tuesday 7 am, Thursday 7 am. Support: Calendar block + accountability text from trainer.

Goal 2: Walk for 20 minutes at lunch on non-training days (Mon, Wed, Fri).
How often: 3x/week. When: Lunch break. Support: Walking shoes kept at desk.

---

**PART 3 — BARRIERS**

What's stopped you before: Work deadlines eat into exercise time. Also start strong, then get bored.

Most likely barrier now: Travel for work in week 3 (3-day trip, no gym confirmed).

Plan B: Hotel room bodyweight routine (trainer will share a 15-min option before the trip).

---

**PART 4 — AGREEMENTS**

What success looks like at 4 weeks: Can play football with kids for 30 minutes without needing to stop, AND has attended at least 6 of 8 planned sessions.

Trainer responsible for: Programming, adapting to travel week, weekly check-in, noting progress.

Tom responsible for: Attending sessions, sending check-in, doing lunch walks when possible.

---

**PART 5 — REVIEW (week 6)**

Progress: On track.

What worked: Tom attended 10 of 12 sessions. The pre-travel bodyweight routine was used. He reported playing with his kids for 45 minutes the previous weekend without stopping.

What needs to change: Lunch walks only happened 50% of the time — Tom says he's not a walker. Replace with 10-minute stretching/mobility routine at his desk instead.

Revised behavior goal 2: Replace walks with a 10-min midday mobility routine (trainer to provide video).

## Key Points on Goal Revision

Revising a goal is not a failure — it's the system working. A goal that gets revised is more honest and more useful than one that gets ignored. Build the revision explicitly into your process (Part 5 of the worksheet) so it feels like a scheduled professional step, not an admission that something went wrong.

If a client's goals change significantly — new injury, life event, major motivation shift — restart the worksheet rather than patching the original. A new situation deserves a new plan.

## Common Pitfalls

**Setting outcome goals without behavior goals.** "Lose 10 kg" is an outcome. Without a behavior plan attached, it's just a wish.

**Setting the trainer's goals, not the client's.** "We'll get you to 15% body fat" is not a co-designed goal. Ask, then reflect back. Don't prescribe what the client should want.

**Goals with no review date.** A goal without a built-in review date lives forever as an unexamined assumption. Put the review date in the calendar when you complete Part 4.

**Catastrophising missed behavior targets.** If a client managed 2 of 3 walks, that's 67% compliance with a new habit — not a failure. Note what's realistic and adjust.
